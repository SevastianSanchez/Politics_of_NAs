Visible States Project

The first transparency measure covering the SDG era: latent reporting behaviour across 524 statistical series and 197 countries, 2015–2023, with uncertainty quantified for every country–year.

Visible States Index (VSI) is the overall score published here, alongside goal–level scores for the 16 SDG goals with enough series to support them.

Existing transparency indices stop in 2015. This one begins there, and resolves reporting by policy domain rather than returning a single national score. Estimated by item response theory on the UN Global SDG Database.

What this measures

Missing data isn’t noise. It’s behaviour.

For every country, series and year, the UN database either holds a published figure or it does not. Treat each of the 524 statistical series as an item on a test and each country–year as a test–taker: a country's underlying willingness and ability to report is then estimated jointly with how demanding each series is. This is the design Hollyer, Rosendorff and Vreeland introduced for World Development Indicators, applied here to SDG data.

The point of the model is that it removes a judgement call. Every simpler measure of missing data has to divide by a denominator chosen by hand — all series in the framework, or only those the UN opened a cell for. The first assumes every country should report what the richest ones do; the second reacts to reporting itself. Seven such measures built for this project disagreed badly, two of them correlating at only 0.36. Estimating each series' difficulty instead of assuming a denominator is what resolves that.

Scores are on a standard–normal scale: 0 is the average country–year, positive is more complete reporting, and each estimate carries its own uncertainty. Concretely, Germany in 2019 scores +1.68, sixteenth of 197 country–years that year; Sierra Leone scores −0.90, 171st. They are posterior means, so the error bars are genuine posterior standard deviations rather than asymptotic approximations.

What this release does and does not do

The attribution layer is the point of the project, and it is not in the scores published here. This page carries the composite index only. Telling withheld data from absent capacity requires the provenance split, which is in progress.

The consequence is visible in the rankings: small island states with genuine capacity constraints currently sit beside governments that are plainly choosing silence. Separating those two is the work, not an oversight in it.

Trend

Average reporting level, year by year

The mean VSI score across all 197 countries in each year. The scale is standard–normal across the whole period: 0 is the average country–year, 2015 to 2023, and one unit is one standard deviation. A year above 0 is one in which the world reported more than that average.

Read 2023 with care

The series rises steadily through 2020 and then falls sharply in 2023. That last drop is very likely an artefact rather than a finding: the UN database fills recent years in gradually, and this snapshot was taken before 2023 had settled. Small states with genuine capacity constraints appear alongside deliberate non–reporters at the bottom of that year for the same reason.

Any analysis using these scores should either drop 2023 or absorb the lag through year effects. Treating it as a normal year would read an archival delay as a collapse in global transparency.

By policy domain

Which areas went quiet

A single national score says a government reports less. It cannot say what it stopped reporting. Scores resolved to the 16 SDG goals with enough series to support them can. And they are not a restatement of the overall index: across the 16 goals, a median of 64% of the variation in goal–level reporting is not explained by a country's overall level, ranging from 18% for goal 17 to almost all of it for goals 6 and 15. The domains move on their own, which is the point of measuring them separately.

Every goal ships with its own error bar

These are model scores, on the same scale as the overall index: positive means a country reported more on that goal than the average country-year, negative means less. Each is a separate fit on that goal's series alone, so a goal's score owes nothing to the other fifteen.

Nothing is excluded for being imprecise. Reliability is printed for every goal and 0.70 is shown as a reference point, not applied as a cut. Two goals are held back on content grounds rather than precision: goal 6 is largely satellite measurement, so “reported” mostly records whether an agency published, and goal 15 carries treaty and policy instruments that may track commitment status rather than reporting effort.

Goal 7 is a different case again, and not the one its score count suggests. The framework gives it only six series, the fewest of any goal and the only goal with no disaggregated series at all. Three of those are reported by more than 98% of country–years — electricity access 99.5%, energy intensity and renewable share 99.0% each — so the filter removes them as carrying no information for separating countries. Three remain, too few to identify a factor. It is listed with no score rather than dropped.

Reading two scores together

The overall score and a goal score answer different questions, and the country–years worth looking at are the ones where they disagree. Two from 2019:

Country–yearOverall RankGoalGoal score
Germany 2019 +1.68 ±0.13 16 of 197 10, reduced inequalities −0.65 ±0.10
Sierra Leone 2019 −0.90 ±0.12 171 of 197 5, gender equality +2.07 ±0.15

Germany reports more than almost any country — sixteenth of 197 that year — and still sits below the average country–year on inequality, where the world median is +0.34. The shortfall is seven standard errors wide, so it is not noise.

Sierra Leone is the mirror image: 171st overall, and near the top of the scale on gender. Reporting little in general does not stop a country reporting heavily on one goal.

Neither case is visible in the overall score alone, which is the argument for publishing the layer. Both goals here are country–produced rather than agency–produced, 5% and 1% agency weight, so the scores reflect what the government published. Check that column before reading a goal score this way: on goals 3 and 2 it is above 90%.

How each goal moved, 2015 to 2023

The bars above are one country in one year. This is every country at once: each goal's mean score across all 197, plotted as change since 2015. The overall index is the dashed line.

Change, not level, because each goal is a separate fit centred on its own mean — so the gap between two goals' lines would say nothing, while the movement within one is real. The list beside the chart runs 1 to 17 in goal order; point at a row or a line to light up both, and click to keep it lit.

Goal 15 moves furthest of all and goal 6 is third, behind goal 16. Both are held on content grounds: goal 6 largely tracks when an agency published a satellite dataset, goal 15 partly tracks treaty ratification. Read those two as publication schedules rather than as countries changing behaviour. The 2023 fall affects goals for the same reason it affects the overall index — the database was still filling that year in when this snapshot was taken.

External validity

Checked against an index built by someone else

A measure that only agrees with itself has proved nothing. These scores are compared with two indices built by other researchers from other evidence. The World Bank’s Statistical Performance Indicators rate a country’s statistical system from censuses, surveys, administrative records and institutional arrangements; agreement is 0.85 across 1,425 shared country–years. The HRV transparency index — the Bayesian latent–variable measure of reporting this method descends from — agrees at 0.85 on the 149 countries where the two overlap.

The ordering is the real result. SPI’s third pillar scores how many SDG indicators a country has published, drawn from the same UN database used here, so some agreement there is mechanical rather than corroborating. If the headline 0.85 were an artifact of shared inputs, pillar 3 would sit at the top. It sits at the bottom, at 0.47, while the pillars built from wholly separate evidence — data sources and data infrastructure — are the ones near 0.82.

What this does not establish

The agreement above is between countries. Within a single country over time, the median correlation with SPI is only 0.15. SPI is a slow–moving assessment of statistical institutions and barely shifts from year to year, so it cannot corroborate the annual movement in these scores — and annual movement is what any analysis of reporting change depends on. Cross–sectional validity is established here; the year–to–year variation is not yet externally checked.

The HRV comparison carries the same limit for a different reason. HRV’s updated release ends in 2015 and this panel begins in 2015, so the two meet in one year only. Its 0.83 is a strong cross–sectional agreement with the closest prior measure of the same thing, and it says nothing about any later year.

Method and status

How it is built, and what is missing

Estimation
Two–parameter logistic item response model, fitted by marginal maximum likelihood with an EM algorithm; scores are expected a posteriori estimates. The reported fit converged rather than exhausting its iteration cap, and reproduced identically on an independent run.
Item set
Two steps, in order. A filter first removes series reported in fewer than 2% or more than 98% of country–years, which carry no information for separating countries: 665 series become 607. Redundant variants are then collapsed, leaving 524.
Why 524 and not 607
Some measurements are entered in the database more than once. Children stunted appears as a percentage and, separately, as a count in thousands. A country that publishes one always publishes the other, so counting both pays it twice for a single reporting decision, while a country whose series has no twin is paid once. Each such cluster therefore contributes one series. Two conditions must both hold before a cluster is collapsed. The series must be reported, and not reported, together in every one of the 1,773 country–years; and their descriptions must show they measure the same thing a different way. The pattern alone is not sufficient, because at these reporting rates unrelated series coincide by chance: forest certification, electricity access and refugee counts all move together in 99.5% of country–years without measuring anything in common. Series that merely resemble one another are kept apart, since a country publishing one and withholding the other has made a real reporting choice. 59 clusters met both conditions. The test is re–run every time the model is fitted and stops it if a future data release breaks it.
Deliberately excluded
No democracy score, income group, population or region enters the model. Those are explanatory variables in the accompanying study, and including them would make any subsequent test circular.
Goal–level scores
Published above, as goal_level. Each goal is a separate fit on its own series rather than one joint model, so a goal's score owes nothing to the other fifteen. Sixteen goals are scored; goal 7 keeps three, too few to identify one: its framework has six series, the fewest of any goal, and half of those are reported by over 98% of country–years and so are filtered out. Fitting on 17–75 series instead of 524 makes these error bars about three times the overall index's, which is why every goal is published with its own.
Not yet smoothed
Scores are estimated independently by year. A dynamic prior linking adjacent years, as in the original transparency index, is deferred: nine years is a short series for a random walk to earn its place.

Roadmap

What each version measures

Three dimensions of reporting behaviour are separable in the SDG database and are being added in sequence. Each is listed with what it covers, because a measure's scope is a fact users need rather than a caveat to be buried.

v1

Domain — this release

Whether a government published anything for each of 524 series, resolved into an overall score and a score per SDG goal. Answers which policy areas fell silent, not merely that a country did. No existing transparency index disaggregates this way.

Every goal score is released with its reliability and standard error beside it, and none is withheld for being imprecise — the convention V–Dem, the World Bank’s governance indicators and the HRV index all follow. Two goals are withheld on a different basis: their series measure something other than a country’s reporting effort, and an interval cannot warn a reader about that.

v2

Provenance

The database records who produced each figure — a national statistical office or an international agency — and why a cell is empty: suppressed, structurally inapplicable, or unexplained. Separating those is what distinguishes a government withholding data from one that cannot produce it. The World Development Indicators record neither, which is why no earlier measure can draw the line.

v3

Depth

A national total and a full breakdown by sex, age and location currently score alike. 36% of series admit disaggregation, and depth correlates with the index at 0.57 once the series set is held constant — so it carries signal the binary discards. It enters as ordered responses the model estimates over, not as a ratio computed after the fact.

Taken together the three produce a measure that can tell silence by choice from silence by incapacity, and locate it by domain. That is the destination; this page documents how far along the way it currently is.
